# LinguaMedica RW reviewer guideline

Version 1.0, September 2026. Written for the reviewers of the September–October 2026 validation round.

## What you are doing, and why

LinguaMedica RW is an English–Kinyarwanda medical dictionary for health professionals in Rwanda, built as a research resource. Each entry gives an English clinical term, a Kinyarwanda rendering, a category, and an example sentence in each language.

Before the resource is released as a validated, citable dataset, every entry in this round is scored independently by two Kinyarwanda reviewers. Your scores decide which entries are released as verified, and the level of agreement between the two reviewers is reported with the resource. That is why the round has two rules that matter more than any other:

- Score alone. Do not discuss individual terms with the other reviewer until the round has closed. If you both score the same term the same way without talking, that agreement means something; if you have talked, it does not.
- Score only what the page shows you. Do not look the term up on the public LinguaMedica site while the round is open. The page you score on is deliberately bare: no author, no source, no etymology, no label saying published or verified. That is so you judge the rendering itself, not its history. English medical references of any kind, and Kinyarwanda references of any kind, are fine to use.

## The question you answer

For every term the page asks one question: how adequately does the Kinyarwanda render the English concept? You answer it with one of four buttons.

## The four levels

**4 · Fully correct and idiomatic.** The rendering is correct, and it is how a Rwandan clinician or a patient would actually say it. A borrowing can be a 4. A descriptive rendering can be a 4.

**3 · Correct meaning, minor issue of form.** The meaning is unmistakable, and something in the form is off: a noun-class agreement, a spelling, a connective, a phrase that reads like a definition where a term would do. A native speaker notices the slip and understands the term anyway. Nothing clinical is lost.

**2 · Partially correct, meaning incomplete.** The rendering is real Kinyarwanda and covers part of the concept, but something has been left out, or it is one step too general for the English term it stands for. A reader could take it to mean something broader, or something adjacent.

**1 · Incorrect or misleading.** The rendering names the wrong thing. It may be well formed and sound confident; that is what makes it dangerous. A clinician who trusted it would act on the wrong condition, organ, or drug.

The line between a 2 and a 1: a 2 leaves something out; a 1 puts something wrong in.

## How to decide

Judge each rendering as a dictionary headword standing alone for the English concept, read by the dictionary's intended reader: a health professional in Rwanda who uses Kinyarwanda with patients and with colleagues.

What counts is whether that reader would understand the rendering correctly and use it naturally. Some things that do not count:

- Your own unfamiliarity with a rendering. If a term is correct but you have not personally met it, that is not a reason to lower the score. Say in the comment that it was new to you, if you like.
- Whether the rendering is borrowed. *Malariya* is a 4. Borrowing is a legitimate strategy, not a failure.
- Whether the rendering is descriptive rather than a single word. *Kubura amaraso* is a 4. Many correct Kinyarwanda medical terms are built from ordinary words.
- Whether you would have chosen a different, equally correct rendering. Score the one shown. Put yours in the alternative rendering field.

A rendering that explains the concept in a sentence instead of naming it is a matter of form: if the meaning is right, score it 3 and say in the comment that it reads as a definition.

If the English term itself is misspelled or unusual, score the Kinyarwanda against the concept the English clearly intends, and note the English problem in the comment.

If you are unsure between two scores, give the lower one and explain in the comment. A comment is worth more to the editor than a generous score.

## Worked anchors, one per score level

Read these before scoring anything. Each shows one distinct kind of failure, so you can place a rendering by asking which of these it most resembles. None of the terms below appears in the scoring queue.

### Score 4. Fully correct and idiomatic

**Anemia** → *Kubura amaraso*
A descriptive rendering: "lacking blood." Correct, natural, and how the condition is named in practice.

**Malaria** → *Malariya*
A borrowing. Also a 4. Borrowing is a legitimate strategy, not a failure. Do not lower a score because a rendering is borrowed rather than built from Kinyarwanda roots. The question is whether it is correct and how a clinician or patient would actually say it.

### Score 3. Correct meaning, minor issue of form

**Bone tuberculosis**
Correct: *Igituntu cy'amagufa*
Score 3: *Igituntu y'amagufa*, or *Igituntu ry'amagufa*

The meaning is unmistakable: tuberculosis of the bone. The fault is grammatical. The connective *cy'* agrees with the noun class of *igituntu*; *y'* and *ry'* belong to other classes. A native speaker notices the slip immediately and understands the term anyway. Nothing clinical is lost. That is a 3.

### Score 2. Partially correct, meaning incomplete or distorted

**Uterine prolapse**
Correct: *Kumurika kwa Nyababyeyi*
Score 2: *Kumurika*

The organ has been dropped. What remains is real Kinyarwanda and does mean prolapse, but prolapse of what is now unspecified, and in clinical use the bare form can refer to other protrusions. The meaning is incomplete rather than wrong. That is a 2.
Note: clinicians say uterine prolapse; patients often say prolapsed cervix when they feel the cervix descending.

### Score 1. Incorrect or misleading

**Stomach ache**
Correct: *Kubabara mu gifu* / *Kurwara igifu*
Score 1: *Kubabara mu kiziba cy'inda*

The rendering is well formed and sounds confident, and it names the wrong organ system. *Igifu* is the stomach, the digestive organ. *Ikiziba cy'inda* is the reproductive tract, the canal through the cervix and uterus. Note that the two renderings share the same frame, *kubabara mu* followed by an organ, so the whole error sits in the organ named. Note also that *inda* inside the phrase would pull a hurried reader toward the abdomen, which is exactly what makes this a convincing wrong answer rather than an obvious one. A reviewer who accepted it would send a digestive complaint to gynaecology. That is a 1: not vague, but precisely wrong.

The line between a 2 and a 1 is the line between the two examples above. A 2 leaves something out. A 1 puts something wrong in.

## Two notes to hold in mind while scoring

### Register: when a polite or general expression stands for a specific condition

Some expressions are genuinely used for a specific condition without naming it. *Kuva* means bleeding, and is used respectfully for menstrual bleeding. *Kubabara mu nda* means abdominal pain, and is used politely for menstrual cramps. Speakers use these correctly in context, and a listener understands.

The cost shows up between professionals, not only between a dictionary and a reader. A doctor tells a nurse, *uyu mukobwa ari kubabara mu nda*. The nurse, moving quickly, starts thinking about the bowel. The doctor sees it and adds, *nshatse kuvuga inda ibyara*, and the nurse nods, because she had been approaching the case the wrong way. Nothing was said incorrectly. The general expression simply left the organ open, and a clinician filled the gap with the wrong one.

You are not scoring conversation. You are scoring a dictionary headword standing alone. Presented as the rendering of a specific condition, a general or euphemistic expression leaves the condition unspecified, and that is a 2. Do not raise it to a 3 because people say it. Do not lower it to a 1 because it is imprecise. Note in the comment field that the form is a common polite usage, so the editor can decide whether to record it as a variant.

### Body site: the specificity the language already carries

*Igifu* is the stomach, the organ. *Inda* is the belly or abdomen, the region. *Inda ibyara* is the reproductive sense. *Ikiziba cy'inda* is the reproductive tract itself, the canal through the cervix and uterus. Kinyarwanda encodes this ladder from region to organ; everyday speech often flattens it to *inda*. When a rendering names a body site, check that it sits on the right rung for the English term. A rendering one rung too general is usually a 2. A rendering on the wrong ladder entirely is a 1.

## Entries that list several forms

Many entries list variants separated by slashes, for example *Impozamarira / Inshumbusho / Igihembo*. Score the set as it is presented, because that is what a user of the dictionary sees. If one variant is weaker than the others, say which in the comment field. Do not score only the first form, and do not pick the strongest one and score that.

## Using the scoring page

**Logging in.** Go to linguamedica.rw/review/login and sign in with the username and password you were given. They are yours alone; please do not share them.

**The queue.** After login the page shows you one term at a time: the English term, the Kinyarwanda rendering, the category, and the example sentences. A counter shows how far through your queue you are. The order is random and fixed for you, so you cannot choose which term comes next, and the other reviewer sees the same terms in a different order.

**Scoring.** Press one of the four buttons. That records your score and moves you to the next term. Before you press, you can fill in two optional fields:

- *Alternative rendering*: if you would render the term differently, type your rendering here. It does not change the score you give to the rendering shown.
- *Comment*: a weak variant in a multi-form entry, a register note, a doubt, an English misspelling, anything the editor should know.

**Skipping.** *Skip for now, come back later* moves the term to the end of your queue without scoring it. Use it when you want to think, or check a reference, and carry on.

**Stopping and resuming.** You can log out at any point. Your place is kept, and the next login shows you the next unscored term.

**Changing a score.** If you return to a term you have already scored, the page asks you to tick *I confirm I want to replace my earlier score for this term* before it accepts a new one. Your earlier score stays in the record; the new one is the one that counts. If, some way into the round, you realise you would now score an early term differently, tell the editor rather than going back through many terms: a note about how your reading shifted is more useful than a rescored batch.

**Finishing.** When the queue is empty the page says *Nothing left to score*. If terms are added to the round later, they appear at your next login.

**Pace.** The round is measured by the count of terms scored, and a steady rhythm matters more than speed. Ten terms a day on the days you can is a good rhythm; at that pace a term takes a minute or two, and the whole round fits into a few weeks of short sessions. It is better to score ten terms with full attention than forty tired ones.

## The pilot

The round opens with a pilot of twenty to thirty terms. Score them as you would any others, then tell the editor what was unclear: a level you could not place, a word in this guideline that did not help, a page that behaved oddly. The guideline may be revised after the pilot; the pilot scores stand.

## Terms withheld from the scoring queue

Because they are used as anchors above, the following entries are excluded from this round: Anemia, Malaria, Tuberculosis, Bone tuberculosis, Uterine prolapse, Stomach ache.

A reviewer is also never shown a term they contributed to the dictionary themselves.

## What happens to your scores

Each score is stored under your reviewer code together with the exact Kinyarwanda string you were shown, so the record can be audited later. The scores of both reviewers decide each entry's validation status and are used to compute the agreement statistics reported with the resource. Individual scores are not published. Disagreements between the two reviewers are looked at after the round, not during it, and you may be asked about a few of them then. You will be credited by name as a reviewer in the released resource and in anything written about it, if you have agreed to be named.

## Questions and problems

Anything at all, from a page that will not load to a term you think is in the wrong language, goes to the editor, Christophe (Khris) Mumaragishyika, at contact@linguamedica.rw or by WhatsApp. Do not wait until the end of the round to mention a problem; a small fix early keeps the round on time.

Thank you for lending the round your judgement. The resource is only as good as the people who check it.
